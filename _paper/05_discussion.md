# 5. Discussion

The results separate into one methodological finding and one engineering outcome. The
methodological finding is that predictive accuracy and decision reliability are distinct
properties of a surrogate, and that the second does not follow from the first (§4.1). The
engineering outcome is that a decision loop built on that distinction — surrogate for scale,
simulator for authority — produced simulator-verified economic gains of +361.3 M US$ over the
historical schedule at 2.7 full-physics evaluations per run (§4.2, §4.3). The role ablation
(§4.4) locates the source of that gain, and the scheduling analysis (§4.5) shows that the
resulting policies are interpretable rather than opaque. We take these in turn, and close with
the boundary of what has and has not been demonstrated.

## 5.1 Predictive accuracy is not decision reliability

The surrogate reproduces the simulator's ordering of randomly sampled schedules almost exactly
(ρ = 0.998, top-1 hit rate 1.0) and its point predictions to 0.369 %. By the validation practice
conventionally used to qualify a surrogate for optimisation, it would be considered ready. Yet on
the ten schedules produced by optimising against that same surrogate, its ordering carries no
usable signal (ρ = −0.21) and its first choice is the simulator's last.

The mechanism is not extrapolation error in the ordinary sense. Point accuracy degrades by a
factor of four (0.369 % → 1.44 %), which alone would not overturn an ordering spread over 1.4 %
of the objective. What overturns it is that the error becomes *correlated with the decision*: the
surrogate's predicted value and its own error correlate at r = 0.97 across the shortlist, and the
candidate it ranks first is the candidate it overestimates most, by a factor of 10.6 against a
shortlist median of 1.66. An optimiser searching against a surrogate does not sample its error
distribution at random; it seeks the region where the surrogate is optimistic. Selecting on the
surrogate therefore selects for surrogate error — the optimiser's curse, here measured directly
against a full-physics arbiter rather than assumed.

Two controls rule out the obvious alternative explanation, that any restriction to top candidates
compresses rank correlation. Drawing the surrogate's own top-10 from the *random* pool preserves
ρ = 0.95 with a top-1 hit rate of 1.0 across a comparable objective spread, and drawing the
simulator's true top-10 from the same pool gives ρ = 0.94. The collapse appears only when the
candidates are generated by optimisation.

The practical implication is a validation gap rather than a modelling defect. A surrogate
validated on an independently sampled test set has been shown to predict well; it has not been
shown to *choose* well among the candidates an optimiser will actually put in front of it. These
are different tests, and only the second is relevant to a decision pipeline. We are not in a
position to claim this as a general property of surrogate-based reservoir optimisation — the
evidence is one field, one surrogate architecture, one optimisation set-up, and an
optimisation-region sample of ten with a wide confidence interval. What the evidence does support
is that high global accuracy did not guarantee reliable decision ranking in the optimiser-generated
region in this case, which is sufficient to make the adjudication step a design requirement rather
than a precaution.

## 5.2 What the adjudication step changes

Given §5.1, the role of the full-physics run in this framework is not verification after the fact.
It is the mechanism that prevents surrogate-generated and agent-generated optimism from being
recorded as economic performance. Every value reported in §4 is a simulator output; no schedule
enters the results on the strength of a surrogate score, and candidates failing the arithmetic
feasibility gate are excluded rather than argued about.

That this is load-bearing rather than decorative is visible in the loop's behaviour. All twenty
runs ended at or above their starting point (§4.3). An earlier configuration of the same framework
— identical surrogate, identical gate, identical simulator — degraded across rounds in six of seven
runs, a net −139.6 M US$. The difference between the two configurations is the adjudication rule
described in §3.4: roles arguing from analogue literature, with no field-specific measurement, had
been weighted equally with roles holding simulated or measured evidence, and were flattening the
main economic axis of the decision. Re-weighting by `source_type` restored the loop's direction.
The feedback structure is therefore not a diagram; changing how adjudication treats evidence
changed the outcome sign.

We are careful about the strength of this claim in two ways. The progression is consistent
end-to-end but not monotone: three of twenty runs finished below their own best. And the
before/after comparison of adjudication rules is not a randomised contrast within a single batch,
so it indicates a mechanism rather than estimating an effect size.

## 5.3 What actually drives the economic gain

The accepted schedules are not arbitrary points in a 24-dimensional space that happened to score
well. Regressing ΔNPV on a single scalar summary of the schedule — the front-loading index τ,
computed on 275 randomly sampled cases with every agent- and optimiser-generated solution excluded
— gives ρ = +0.714, and the accepted agent schedules sit on the positive side of that axis
(τ = +0.66 for the team arm, +0.92 for the single-agent arm).

The direction itself is unsurprising: injecting earlier rather than later serves both sweep
efficiency and discounting, and a reservoir engineer would expect it. We regard that as a
strength rather than a weakness of the result. It means a high-dimensional policy produced by
language-model reasoning compresses, in substantial part, onto a direction that is legible in
reservoir terms — the framework did not earn its gain through an opaque exploit that an operator
would be unable to sanction. The finding to report is therefore not a new reservoir law but the
presence of interpretable economic structure inside an AI-generated high-dimensional decision.

This remains an association. τ was not intervened upon, no identification assumptions are stated,
and no cross-field evidence is offered; the analysis explains part of the observed gain rather
than establishing a transferable rule.

## 5.4 Role decomposition is not the dominant source of performance

The seven-role team exceeded the single-role arm by +13.8 M US$, which this experiment cannot
resolve (Welch p = 0.63; Mann–Whitney p = 0.52; 95 % CI [−45.2, +72.8]; Cohen's *d* = 0.22). With
ten repetitions per arm and a run-to-run standard deviation of roughly 50–70 M US$, the experiment
is not powered to detect a difference of this size, and the result is a statement about
detectability rather than about equivalence. The team arm's lower dispersion (SD 50.7 versus
72.9 M US$) is likewise not resolvable (Levene p = 0.45) and is reported descriptively.

Read together with §4.2, the ablation is informative precisely because it is negative. The
improvement over the reference strategy is large and highly significant; the further decomposition
of the reasoning into seven specialised roles, with a fivefold larger language-model budget and an
additional cross-examination stage, is not separable from run-to-run variation. The gain is
attributable to the closed loop — structured proposal, cheap screening, arithmetic feasibility,
physical adjudication, feedback — rather than to the number of reasoning roles.

For practice this carries a warning that we think is worth stating plainly. Elaborating an agent
architecture increases cost and reduces auditability, and should not be assumed to improve
reservoir decisions merely because it increases the amount of reasoning structure. In this case
study, one role with the same loop around it performed indistinguishably from seven.

## 5.5 Boundary of the evidence

**Reference strategy.** The no-agent control is one-dimensional uniform scaling, an intentionally
constrained reference rather than a state-of-the-art optimiser benchmark. It quantifies the
economic gain obtained by moving from simple global adjustment to structured high-dimensional
decision making. Two factors change together across that comparison — the presence of an agent and
the expansion from one to twenty-four decision variables — and the experiment does not separate
them. Accordingly we do not claim superiority over conventional reservoir optimisation methods;
no such optimiser was benchmarked here.

**Sample size.** Ten repetitions per arm support the large effect against the reference strategy
but not fine discrimination between the agent arms, and support no claim of equivalence.

**Single field, single configuration.** All results are from the Norne model under one economic
setting, one surrogate architecture and one optimisation set-up. The surrogate ranking collapse of
§5.1 in particular has not been reproduced across surrogates or fields.

**No human baseline.** The framework is compared with algorithmic references only; nothing here
speaks to how it compares with an experienced reservoir engineer.

**Interpretation, not identification.** τ is an association (§5.3). The influence matrix of §4.6 is
a partial regression on simulated cases, not a tracer measurement.

**Historical field.** Norne's production history has already occurred. The economic figures are
counterfactual evaluations within a validated full-physics model, and are not, and cannot be,
field-validated outcomes. The contribution offered here is the decision framework and its
evidence, with the field serving as the test bed.
